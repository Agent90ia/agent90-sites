What Is ICM in Poker?

ICM stands for Independent Chip Model. It's the standard math used to convert a player's tournament chip stack into real prize-pool dollars (their "equity") at any point in a tournament or Spin&Go, based on everyone's current stack and the remaining payout structure. ICM answers a question chip counts alone can't: if the tournament ended right now under skill-neutral probabilities, how much is your stack actually worth in cash?

Why Chip Count Isn't the Same as Prize Equity

Take this calculator's own default example: four players with 1,000 / 800 / 600 / 400 chips (2,800 total) playing a 50/30/20 payout split. The chip leader holds 35.7% of the chips but only about 31.3% of the equity — while the shortest stack holds 14.3% of the chips but about 17.2% of the equity. A short stack is worth more than its chip share, and a big stack worth less, because busting out is final but a small stack can still ladder into a bigger payout. That gap between chip% and equity% is the entire reason ICM exists, and it's why maximizing chips (chipEV) can be a costly mistake near the money bubble or at an unbalanced final table, where protecting your current payout spot is often worth more than winning extra chips.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between chipEV and $EV (ICM)?

ChipEV treats every chip as equally valuable regardless of stack size or payout structure. $EV (ICM) accounts for the fact that losing your last chip busts you for good, while a min-stack can still ladder into a bigger payout — so a chip-neutral or even chip-losing decision can be $EV-positive near the money, and a chip-gaining all-in can be $EV-negative.

When does ICM matter most?

ICM pressure is strongest near the money bubble, at unbalanced final tables (especially heads-up or three-handed with big stack differences), and in Spin&Go-style top-heavy payout formats, where protecting your current spot is often worth more than a marginal chip gain.

Is ICM equity an exact prediction?

No — it's a skill-neutral mathematical baseline that assumes equal win probability per chip. It ignores real factors like stack-specific skill edges, table position and ICM-aware opponents, but it's the standard reference point serious tournament and Spin&Go decisions are measured against.
